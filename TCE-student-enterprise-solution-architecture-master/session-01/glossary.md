# Glossary — Session 1

## How to use this glossary

Use this page when a word stops you. Do not memorize every definition. Read the plain explanation, connect it to the Priya example, and return to the lesson.

Plain-English meanings for the terms used in Day 1. Only terms introduced tonight are listed here — later sessions add their own new words as they come up.

**Business as Usual (BAU)**
The organization's normal daily work continuing reliably, every day. A bank processing transactions, a college registering students — BAU means these keep happening without interruption.

**Enterprise system**
A collection of people, processes, applications, data, and technology, all working together to run a company's operations — not one program, but everything around it that makes the program useful in a real business.

**Architecture** *(in this course's sense)*
The reasoning and decisions behind how a company's technology is organized — why it looks the way it does, not just what it is. Someone decided, ahead of time, how the pieces fit together; that decision-making is architecture.

**Application**
A piece of software built to do a specific job — like the systems used to check a customer's account balance or process an employee's payroll.

**Data**
The information a company collects, stores, and relies on — customer records, transaction history, employee details. Data usually outlives the specific application that first created it.

**Infrastructure**
The physical and digital foundation that software runs on — servers, storage, networks, data centers, and cloud services.

**IT asset**
Any piece of technology a company owns and tracks — a laptop, a phone, a server, a software license. Companies track IT assets the way they track money: who has it, what it costs, and when it needs replacing.

**Governance**
Agreed rules and decision processes so technology is purchased, built, secured, operated, and retired responsibly — instead of every team deciding on its own.

**Availability**
A system being ready and working when people need it. If a college's payment portal goes down on the final fee-payment day, students can't pay. In a bank, an unavailable system can stop thousands of transactions at once. That's why enterprise systems are designed to keep running even when individual parts fail.

**Security**
Making sure only the right people can access the right information and systems, and protecting those systems from being misused or broken into.

**Monitoring**
Watching systems continuously so that problems are noticed quickly — ideally before a customer ever sees them.

**Backup**
A saved copy of data, kept separately, so that information isn't lost forever if something fails or gets deleted by mistake.

**Disaster recovery**
The plan a company has for getting its systems working again after a serious failure — a data center outage, a natural disaster, a major security incident.
